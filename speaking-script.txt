WHO OWNS HISTORY?
The Debate Over Museum Treasures

SPEAKING SCRIPT FOR TWO PRESENTERS
Presenter A: slides 1, 3, 5, 7
Presenter B: slides 2, 4, 6, 8
Target: about 6 minutes, including short pauses and handovers.
The slide titles below are cues, not part of the spoken text.

SLIDE 1 | THE QUESTION | PRESENTER A

Imagine that an important part of your country's history is in a museum far away. You can see it online, but visiting it is difficult. Who should decide where it belongs?

Our topic is “Who Owns History? The Debate Over Museum Treasures”. Our main question is: should these treasures return to their countries of origin?

We will look at two examples, compare the arguments, and suggest a fair way forward.

SLIDE 2 | WHY OBJECTS MATTER | PRESENTER B

A museum object can be beautiful, but its meaning goes beyond art. It can tell us about a community's beliefs and everyday life. It can also help people feel connected to their past.

When an object moves to another country, the museum chooses how to explain it. That gives the museum power over the story.

So this debate is about memory and identity as well as ownership. The question is also: whose voice do visitors hear?

SLIDE 3 | THE BENIN BRONZES | PRESENTER A

Our first example is the Benin Bronzes, from a kingdom in present-day Nigeria.

In 1897, British troops captured Benin City. The royal palace was looted, and thousands of objects were taken away. Many later entered museums and private collections.

These artworks helped record royal history and traditions. Removing them meant that local people lost access to part of their own past.

This example matters because the objects were taken by force. A later purchase does not remove that history.

SLIDE 4 | THE PARTHENON SCULPTURES | PRESENTER B

Our second example is the Parthenon sculptures from Athens.

In the early eighteen hundreds, Lord Elgin removed sculptures from the building. He said he had permission from the Ottoman authorities, who controlled Athens at the time. In 1816, Britain bought the collection after a parliamentary inquiry accepted his claim.

Greece challenges the removal and wants the separated parts brought together.

This case is different from Benin. The debate includes a claim of legal permission. But permission from a ruling power may not mean agreement from local people.

SLIDE 5 | THE CASE FOR KEEPING | PRESENTER A

There are serious arguments for keeping treasures in major museums.

Visitors can learn about different cultures in one place. Museums also care for objects and support research. Seeing works from different societies together can help us notice connections.

These benefits matter, and returning every object would not automatically solve every problem.

However, we should ask who can actually visit. An international collection may be open to everyone, but travel can be expensive for people from the objects' countries of origin.

SLIDE 6 | THE CASE FOR RETURNING | PRESENTER B

The strongest argument for returning objects is fairness. People should have a chance to experience their own cultural history close to home.

If a family lost an important object, we would understand why they wanted it back. Communities can feel the same way.

Original surroundings also help explain an object's meaning. This is especially important when separated pieces belong to one monument.

Still, each case needs evidence. We should not assume that every object in a foreign museum was stolen.

SLIDE 7 | A WAY FORWARD | PRESENTER A

Returning ownership and sharing culture can work together.

In 2022, the Smithsonian transferred ownership of twenty-nine Benin objects to Nigeria. The agreement also allowed nine to stay at the museum on loan.

This shows that an object can have a recognised owner and still reach an international audience.

Our suggestion is to check how objects were collected, agree on ownership, and arrange loans when both sides want them. Local communities should help make these decisions. Museums can also continue to research the objects together.

SLIDE 8 | OUR ANSWER | PRESENTER B

Our view is that clearly looted objects should be returned. In disputed cases, museums should share the evidence and work towards an agreement.

At the same time, we support loans and cultural exchange. Returning an object does not have to end a relationship between museums. It can make that relationship fairer.

History belongs to people, and their voices should be part of the story.

Thank you for listening. What would a fair museum look like to you?

THREE WORDS FROM THE VOCABULARY SCREENSHOTS

troops = soldiers as a group
captured = took control of a place
looted = stole objects during war or an attack

All three appear on slide 3 and in Presenter A's script.
“Looted” appears again in the conclusion.

THREE GRAMMAR FEATURES

1. Past Simple: “British troops captured Benin City.”
   Use: a completed event in the past.

2. Passive voice: “The royal palace was looted.”
   Use: focus on what happened to the palace.

3. Second conditional: “If a family lost an important object,
   we would understand why they wanted it back.”
   Use: imagine a situation and its result.
